# Who gets held accountable?

<div class="shots tall">
<figure><img style="max-height: 680px" src="illustrations/accountability--ibm-manual.jpeg" alt="A photo of a slide from a 1979 IBM presentation: 'A computer can never be held accountable. Therefore a computer must never make a management decision.'"><figcaption><a href="https://knowyourmeme.com/memes/a-computer-can-never-be-held-accountable">An IBM training slide, 1979 (via Know Your Meme)</a></figcaption></figure>
</div>

<p class="lede fragment">The operator acts. Accountability does not transfer.</p>

Note:
Third and final payoff of the spine. Anything you can specify, the system can do -- and you specified it.

A slide from an IBM presentation in 1979: "A computer can never be held accountable. Therefore a computer must never make a management decision." Forty-plus years later, computers can make a lot of decisions. The first half of the slide hasn't changed.

(Click.) The operator acts; the accountability does not transfer to the tool. You can't say "the AI did it" any more than "the spreadsheet did it." You pressed send. You signed it.

This is the convergence point: whatever your stance, you own what you sign. Do not cut this beat.

Tone: serious but not preachy -- an empowering claim, not a scolding.

Bridge: "So let's practice. Would you put your name on this?"

Note:
Put it on screen. "Take a minute. The fish produced this. It looks professional. Would you put your name on it?" Pause; let someone find the date ("Octoober").

(Click.) "What else would you check?" (Click.) "How long would that take?"

(Click.) The fish can make this in seconds. Checking it, and deciding to put your name on it, still takes a person. Accountability is the new bottleneck.

The error is deliberately small and findable in seconds once anyone looks: the lesson is that polish suppresses looking, not that the error was hard to find. The self-describing title is the wink -- everything on the page is surface. If the room finds it instantly, pivot: "That's the cover. The report behind it is 30 pages."

Exercise: evaluate the artifact. Socratic: "How would you check?" Cognitive shift: "who's responsible?" -> "I am." Stance: convergence -- this is where the enthusiast and the skeptic meet.

<p class="lede">Draft = the fish works. <span class="fragment">Send = you own it. <span class="term">human in the loop</span></span></p>

Note:
Callback to station 8: the difference between "draft this email" and "send this email" is the entire accountability question in one toggle.

One draft is harmless. (Click.) One is a disaster -- and it's exactly what the poisoned water in the mailbox exercise would produce (station 9).

(Click.) The only thing between the second one and the outbox is that toggle -- and the person who reads the drafts.

(Click.) Human-in-the-loop isn't an afterthought; it's a deliberate design choice. Drafts-only is a wall. Who pressed send?

Method: HITL (human in the loop) as a deliberate pattern.

# Who checks the fish?

<div class="placeholder">TODO: review-ladder visual (not a literal ladder) -- four levels of review building up, ending in a person</div>

1. Self-review: same blind spots <!-- .element: class="fragment" -->
2. Another fish: shared blind spots <!-- .element: class="fragment" -->
3. An adversary: genuinely independent <!-- .element: class="fragment" -->
4. It ends with a person. <!-- .element: class="fragment" -->

Note:
Walk the review ladder; let them find the regress.

(Click.) Self-review: asking the fish to check its own work. Same blind spots.

(Click.) A second fish with similar training: correlated review. Shared blind spots. (A black-and-white artist judging black-and-white art.)

(Click.) Adversarial review: a genuinely independent reviewer, told to find what's wrong -- a color artist judging the black-and-white art. Better. (Subagents return here as adversarial reviewers.)

(Click.) But it terminates in a person. And human review rubber-stamps when it's tired. When judgment itself is automated, the signals that survive are the ones costly to fake: provenance, track record, exposure to consequences.

Methods: the review ladder, adversarial subagents.
